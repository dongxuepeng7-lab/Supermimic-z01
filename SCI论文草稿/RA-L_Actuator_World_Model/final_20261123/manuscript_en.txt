Actuator-Structured World Models for Bipedal Motion Tracking:
Prediction, Response, and Control

Abstract— Differentiable world models expose a controller
to errors in both predicted motion and command sensitivity.
We study an actuator-structured transition for the 26-joint
Z01 biped and distinguish these two errors from closedloop performance. The framework combines identiﬁed joint
parameters, an explicit effort channel, and paired ﬁniteamplitude response diagnostics. An analysis of branch errors
and perturbation noise explains why improved trajectory ﬁt
need not yield better response or control. Archived parameterset comparisons improve angle, velocity, and torque prediction
on seven of eight joint records. A current online study completes
31 matched training-seed pairs, each with 5000 policy and
25000 world-model updates. Mean completion on 163 reference
clips is 89.11%/89.85% for the matched/structured models
on the 4090 cohort and 88.90%/87.79% on the 5080 cohort.
Mean paired calibration angle-error reductions are 3.09% and
5.30%, respectively, without consistent control improvement.
Full CPU/GPU checks fail on 65 and 67 of 516 response blocks
in two independent processes. These ﬁndings support a forwardprediction beneﬁt in the studied setting while identifying response validity and policy optimization as unresolved conditions
for stronger control claims.

The paper makes three contributions. First, it speciﬁes a

ﬁt to response supervision, and states precisely what such
supervision can and cannot guarantee. Third, it reports a
staged empirical assessment: archived joint replay, online
policy training, independent randomized policy evaluation,
and full response-backend checks. A response-calibrated
horizon mechanism is formulated as a conditional extension;
its controller beneﬁt is not claimed as an executed result.

A. Actuation and Transfer

I. I NTRODUCTION
Motion imitation offers a practical route to diverse wholebody behavior: reference tracking, learned motion priors,
and humanoid teleoperation have substantially expanded the
skills obtainable from human motion data [1], [2], [3].
Differentiable world models offer a complementary way to
train the controller, replacing expensive imagined physical
rollouts with a learned transition and propagating gradients
through that transition [4]. The resulting controller depends
not only on where the model predicts the robot will move,
but also on how the predicted motion changes when a joint
command changes.
This distinction is particularly relevant to positioncontrolled bipeds. Proportional–derivative (PD) gains, effort
limits, damping, and friction determine the local route from
action to joint acceleration. A network can ﬁt frequently
observed motion while assigning an inaccurate derivative to
this route. Conversely, imposing actuator structure can reduce
forward error without improving optimization through a long,
contact-dependent rollout. Joint calibration, response ﬁdelity,
and closed-loop control therefore require separate evidence.
We investigate this separation in a local PartyMimic Z01
implementation, using the differentiable-world-model training paradigm of SuperMimic [4]. Our main comparison holds
network input width, parameter count, initial actor/critic,
and update budgets ﬁxed while replacing a raw-action input

a local fork with explicit modiﬁcations, not an unmodiﬁed
reproduction of that system or its reported efﬁciency.

D. A Conditional Training-Loss Result
Let F be a common forward ﬁtting loss and G a ﬁxed
response ﬁtting loss over the same model family and data.
Assume attained global minima θB ∈ arg min F and θC ∈
arg min(F + λG), with λ > 0. Optimality implies


λ G(θC ) − G(θB ) ≤ F (θB ) − F (θC ) ≤ 0.
(8)
The ﬁrst inequality follows by comparing C’s objective to its
value at θB ; the second follows from B’s optimality. Thus
response ﬁtting loss cannot increase under these assumptions,
but forward ﬁtting loss can. Finite optimization, changed
online data, and changed loss deﬁnitions break the premise.
In particular, a Huber ﬁtting objective does not guarantee
a smaller reported MSE, and neither objective guarantees
calibration error or controller return. This conditional result
explains a possible ﬁtting trade-off without assigning theoretical values to unmeasured experiments.

A. Platform, Data, and Derived Quantities
The platform is the 26-joint Z01 with 163 retargeted reference clips derived from LAFAN1 [17], including locomotion
and other motion categories. Training uses GPU physics
based on MuJoCo [18]. The control interval is 20 ms and
the physics interval is 5 ms. The response dataset contains
400 ﬁt blocks from 23 source sessions, 116 calibration
blocks from seven sessions, and 136 locked test blocks from
seven sessions. Sessions are disjoint across these response
splits. Policy training uses the full reference library, so
these partitions do not deﬁne held-out policy motions. Test
response arrays were not accessed.

C. Metrics and Evidence Provenance
Each policy evaluation starts every reference clip at its
ﬁrst frame with evaluation seed 0 and the identiﬁed plant.
Completion requires reaching the reference end without
crossing the training termination thresholds: 0.25 m absolute
root-height error or 0.8 absolute Rzz error relative to the
reference. These are tracking-termination criteria, not direct
labels of physical falling. Calibration angle RMSE pools both
branches, joints, and the 32-step preﬁx; ER is deﬁned by (5).
Reported standard deviations are across training seeds, not
individual frames.

V. R ESULTS
A. Archived Joint Replay
Table IV reports the eight available archived records. All
three RMSE channels improve on seven records. Left-knee
position error decreases from 7.400 to 3.680 mrad; left-hiproll error decreases from 13.861 to 3.192 mrad. Right-anklepitch position error decreases from 4.415 to 2.141 mrad,
while its speed and torque gains are smaller. Figure 2 uses
ﬁxed early windows of these records rather than selected best
intervals.
Left-ankle-roll position error increases from 7.764 to
9.116 mrad, accompanied by worse speed and torque prediction. Recorded positions cross the model’s lower limit,
and an archived limit-disabled diagnostic reduces absolute
errors without reversing the original/identiﬁed ordering. The
cause is not established. These are within-record parameter
comparisons with one record per condition; they do not
isolate event-timing identiﬁcation or demonstrate learnedpolicy transfer. Raw ﬁtting sessions and matched timing reﬁts
remain unavailable.
B. From Frozen-Data Failure to Online Control
In the historical common-seed evaluation, A0/A attain
identiﬁed-plant completion of 79.14 ± 2.74% and 79.51 ±
2.24% over ﬁve training seeds. A+, B, and two C candidates
each complete none of the 163 clips in every seed, with
median valid durations approximately 1.00–1.02 s. These are
genuine observed failures, not missing entries or theoretical
lower bounds. We retain the result here rather than repeating
a table of identical zero percentages.
Online recovery subsequently yields primary A+ completion of 136/163 and 133/163 for seeds 2 and 4, against
127/163 and 129/163 for A in that recovery experiment.
A separate matched 1000-update structure experiment yields
A+/B completion of 133/132 and 131/130 clips on the common 3090. Recovery establishes that the matched-input path
can support policy learning, but does not identify a unique
cause of the earlier failure. These small studies motivate,
rather than substitute for, the larger current comparison.

only about 0.20% and 0.21% lower than the zero-response

D. Independent Policy and Response Checks
The 3090 randomized evaluation uses the four earlier
1000-update models, not the 62 new policies. Fourteen
complete evaluation seeds under each of two plants produce 28 balanced four-model cells. Training-range dynamics
randomization and default training observation noise are
enabled, with matched initial scenario hashes. Table VI
shows negative mean B-minus-A+ completion in all four
condition/training-seed combinations. There are still only
two training seeds. Three additional completed evaluations in
an incomplete cell are retained but excluded from balanced
aggregation; its fourth model is interrupted at the eight-hour
limit. This is training-distribution sensitivity, not held-out
robustness.
Two independent 3090 processes check every ﬁt/cal response block at three amplitudes and 4/8-step horizons. The
engineering tolerances are ﬁxed beforehand: scaled forward
RMSE at most 0.1; CPU/GPU response discrepancy at most
the greater of 0.1 and 10% of CPU response RMS; repeat
discrepancy at most the greater of 0.01 and 1% of CPU
response RMS. Both CPU scenes match exactly, archived
CPU replay differs by at most 5.61 × 10−13 , and initial-state
transfer passes. However, 65/516 and 67/516 blocks fail at
least one prescribed check (Table VII); 15/516 also fail crossprocess response repeatability. Recalculation from saved raw
trajectories agrees with these counts.
This localizes a numerical inconsistency to the backend/repeatability part of the response pipeline, without proving a single solver, precision, or contact cause. The original
failed blocks are retained and thresholds are not relaxed.

VI. D ISCUSSION AND C ONCLUSION
The empirical pattern agrees with the scope of the analysis: actuator structure can improve trajectory prediction
while branch-response error and policy performance remain
unresolved. In the older frozen-data study, C with MSE
weight 10−4 reduces mean ﬁtting ER (8) from B’s 69.978 to
14.014, yet calibration error increases from 75.323 to 76.136.
A Huber candidate reaches 74.368, still above the zeroresponse calibration baseline 73.893. Equation (8) concerns
a common ﬁtting objective under global optimization and
does not predict these calibration outcomes. Equations (6)–
(7) explain why a small forward error is insufﬁcient; they do
not identify the experimental failure’s unique cause.
Several further links require direct tests. In (2), command

derivatives vanish outside the unsaturated PD range, while
the smooth friction slope is largest near zero velocity. Their
effect on optimization is a hypothesis requiring measured
saturation, response, and gradient diagnostics. Shorter or
calibrated horizons may reduce accumulated sensitivity error,
but no completion value for D or C-perm follows from an
equal imagined-step budget. A gradient-swap control with
ﬁxed forward values could isolate backward-model effects,
following the decoupling principle in [16]; it has not been
executed here.
The current study has traceable budgets and broad seed
coverage, but remains developmental: calibration data informed screening, current model weights have not been independently transferred, and current evaluations use trainingreference motions. The historical hardware evidence is
archived joint replay rather than whole-body deployment.
No matched PPO efﬁciency comparison, held-out actuator
robustness, event-timing reﬁt advantage, or time-to-target
beneﬁt is established. Unexecuted outcomes are not entered
as zeros or replaced with ideal theoretical values.
In conclusion, identiﬁed parameters improve seven of
eight archived joint records, and the online structured model
lowers calibration angle error in 24 of 31 paired seeds.
Current completion is approximately 88–90%, superseding
the frozen-data failure as the description of the active implementation. The inconsistent closed-loop difference and
full response-backend failures prevent a claim of stable
control improvement. The resulting contribution is a tested
separation of prediction, response, and control evidence,
together with conditional analysis that identiﬁes what must
be validated before response-calibrated policy optimization
can be supported.
